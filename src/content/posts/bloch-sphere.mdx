We have a precise description of a qubit: a unit vector $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ in a two-dimensional complex vector space. Two complex numbers. Four real degrees of freedom.

Four dimensions is too many to picture directly. But two constraints reduce this to something you can actually visualize — and the result turns out to be the surface of a sphere.

## Counting Degrees of Freedom

Start with four real numbers: $\text{Re}(\alpha)$, $\text{Im}(\alpha)$, $\text{Re}(\beta)$, $\text{Im}(\beta)$.

**Constraint 1: normalization.** The state must satisfy $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. This removes one degree of freedom, leaving three.

**Constraint 2: global phase.** This one is subtler. Quantum mechanics has a structural property: multiplying an entire state by $e^{i\theta}$ for any real $\theta$ produces a state that is physically indistinguishable from the original. No measurement, now or ever, can tell the difference between $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\theta}|\psi\rangle$.

This is not an approximation. It is exact, and it follows from how probabilities are computed. Measurement outcomes depend on $|\text{amplitude}|^2$, and $|e^{i\theta} \cdot \alpha|^2 = |\alpha|^2$ — the global phase factor cancels.

Global phase is physically meaningless, so we can always choose a convention that removes it. By convention, we choose $\alpha$ to be a real, non-negative number. This removes another degree of freedom.

We are left with **two real degrees of freedom** to describe any physically distinct single-qubit state.

Two free parameters — exactly the number needed to index the surface of a sphere. The parametrization we are about to write bakes normalization in automatically, so the two degrees of freedom correspond directly to angular coordinates: a polar angle $\theta$ and an azimuthal angle $\varphi$.

## The Parametrization

Write the state in a form that makes the two degrees of freedom explicit:

$$|\psi\rangle = \cos\!\tfrac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\!\tfrac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle$$

where $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ and $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$.

The factor of $\theta/2$ is deliberate. At $\theta = 0$: $\cos(0) = 1$, $\sin(0) = 0$, so $|\psi\rangle = |0\rangle$. At $\theta = \pi$: $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$, $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$, so $|\psi\rangle = e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle$, which is physically $|1\rangle$ (global phase). The factor of 2 maps the full range of qubit states onto a complete sphere without wrapping around twice.

Interpret $\theta$ as the polar angle from the north pole, and $\varphi$ as the azimuthal angle. Every qubit state is now a point on a unit sphere. This is the **Bloch sphere**.

## Reading the Sphere

**North pole ($\theta = 0$):** $|\psi\rangle = |0\rangle$. Probability of measuring 0 is $\cos^2(0) = 1$. Certainty.

**South pole ($\theta = \pi$):** $|\psi\rangle = |1\rangle$. Probability of measuring 1 is $\sin^2(\pi/2) = 1$. Certainty.

**Equator ($\theta = \pi/2$):** $|\psi\rangle = (|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$. Equal probability of measuring 0 or 1, for any $\varphi$. The four cardinal points on the equator are named:

- $|{+}\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ — positive x-axis ($\varphi = 0$)
- $|{-}\rangle = (|0\rangle - |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ — negative x-axis ($\varphi = \pi$)
- $|{+i}\rangle = (|0\rangle + i|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ — positive y-axis ($\varphi = \pi/2$)
- $|{-i}\rangle = (|0\rangle - i|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ — negative y-axis ($\varphi = 3\pi/2$)

These four states all produce a 50/50 measurement outcome. They are nonetheless distinct physical states that behave differently under operations.

## Measurement as Projection

When you measure a qubit in the standard basis — the z-axis of the Bloch sphere — the state collapses to one of the two poles. The probabilities follow directly from the parametrization:

- Probability of measuring 0 (north pole): $\cos^2(\theta/2)$
- Probability of measuring 1 (south pole): $\sin^2(\theta/2)$

A state close to the north pole has small $\theta$, so $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ is close to 1. The closer a state is to a pole, the more certain the corresponding measurement outcome. Distance from the pole is geometric uncertainty.

After measurement, the state is no longer $|\psi\rangle$. It is either $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$, collapsed to the corresponding pole. The original superposition is gone. This is why you cannot extract both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from a single measurement — you only ever see which pole the state fell to.

## Phase Is Invisible — For Now

Notice that $\varphi$ does not appear anywhere in the measurement probabilities. The azimuthal angle — the longitude of the Bloch sphere — has no effect on what you observe when you measure.

This might suggest that phase is unimportant. It is not.

Phase becomes observable the moment you apply a gate before measuring. A gate is a rotation of the Bloch sphere. A rotation that moves $\varphi$ can change $\theta$ — and changing $\theta$ changes the measurement probabilities. Two states that are indistinguishable by direct measurement can become distinguishable after a rotation, because the rotation treats different $\varphi$ values differently.

This is quantum interference. It is the mechanism behind every quantum algorithm, and it is entirely a consequence of phase. We will explore it carefully in post 4, after we have a concrete gate to work with.
